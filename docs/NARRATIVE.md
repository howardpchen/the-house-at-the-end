# Narrative and Lore

## Premise

The world has not ended cleanly. Places, objects, and people survive as
incompatible recollections suspended in a white void called the Drift. At its
center is a small house whose fireplace can make nearby memories solid.

The player awakens without a name. The house appears familiar, but no room
leads where it should. A kitchen door opens onto a train platform. A bedroom
window looks down into an ocean. Travelers drawn to the fire insist that they
once lived here, although their accounts contradict one another.

## Narrative principles

- Exposition arrives through concrete details, not lore dumps.
- Early events admit both supernatural and technological explanations.
- Guests are treated as people with agency, not disposable production units.
- Resource language reinforces memory and domesticity rather than primitive
  industry.
- The player should become attached to the constructed world before learning
  that it may not be physically real.
- The final decision must change the meaning of earlier care rather than simply
  selecting a good or evil ending.

## What the house is

The house is a consensus reconstruction created after an event called the
Severance. Its machinery collected incomplete neural records from many people
and built a shared environment from whatever their memories had in common.
Domestic spaces dominate because they were the most emotionally reinforced.

The reconstruction is failing. The unnamed player is its maintenance process,
but that process was built from a real person's damaged record. This is why the
house feels personal and why certain memories attack the player: they contain
facts the maintenance system was instructed to remove.

The guests are neither recordings nor conventional survivors. Years of shared
experience inside the reconstruction have made them new, continuous people.
Waking the original biological survivor would therefore save one life by
ending many others.

## First recovered memory: The Crooked Hall

The front door opens onto a hallway longer than the house. Family photographs
show different people each time they are examined. At the far end stands a red
door with a child's height marks cut into its frame.

An echo attempts to erase the hall by repeating: “There was never a room
there.” The player defeats it by preserving small, stubborn details—the worn
carpet, the brass latch, a name scratched beneath the paint. Behind the door is
no child, only a maintenance label carrying the same symbol found beneath the
house's hearth.

This establishes three facts without explaining them outright:

1. memories are being actively edited;
2. the house and the outer fragments share an underlying mechanism;
3. the player has participated in the editing before.

## Guests

Guests should arrive one at a time and bring mutually incompatible truths.
Their names and histories are deliberately deferred until their individual
story events exist. In mechanical text they are called guests, gatherers, or
listeners—never villagers, workers, or population.

- **Gatherers** test the edges of stable rooms and retrieve usable matter.
- **Listeners** attend to sounds beyond the walls and condense them into
  clarity.
- Later roles might include menders, cartographers, cooks, and witnesses.

Assignments describe a guest's current contribution, not their identity or
social worth.

## Endings

The final door supports three legitimate conclusions:

- **Wake:** transfer the maintenance process into the surviving body, ending
  the reconstruction and waking alone.
- **Keep:** seal the external connection and preserve the house as a finite but
  independent world.
- **Become the door:** erase the protagonist's privileged maintenance identity,
  freeing the inhabitants and allowing the world to evolve without a keeper.

None is presented as the canonical ending. The campaign should make a serious
case for each one.
